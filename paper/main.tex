\section*{Overview}
%% ============================================================

This report documents a series of analyses comparing extracellular space (ECS) morphology between chemical fixation and rapid high-pressure freezing (HPF) preparations in the CellMap open-access volume EM dataset. The goal is to provide a thorough, reproducible account of what was measured, how it was measured, what the results were, and what biases and artifacts affect each measurement. This is intended as a companion reference for a larger manuscript effort, not as a standalone publication.

The analysis covers 42 ground-truth annotated crops from 8 datasets spanning 4 mouse tissues (kidney, heart, liver, cortex) and 2 preparation methods. Nine metrics were computed; one was discarded as uninformative and one was superseded by an improved method. All analysis scripts, output CSVs, and plots are available in the project repository.

%% ============================================================
\section*{Data}
%% ============================================================

\subsection*{Source}
All data were obtained from the CellMap open-access volume EM collection \cite{Heinrich2021, Xu2021}, accessed via SMB mount. Segmentation masks were read directly from zarr arrays at path \texttt{\{dataset\}/\{dataset\}.zarr/recon-1/labels/groundtruth/\{crop\}}. Two label types were used: (1) a semantic binary ECS mask (0 = not ECS, 1 = ECS) and (2) an instance cell segmentation mask (0 = no cell, nonzero integer = cell ID).

Voxel sizes were read from zarr array metadata and ranged from 2\,nm to 8\,nm isotropic. Table~\ref{tab:datasets} lists all datasets, tissues, preparation types, voxel sizes, and crop counts.

\subsection*{Critical limitation: voxel size is confounded with preparation type}
All Chemical fixation crops were imaged at 2\,nm (7 crops, cortex) or 4\,nm (14 crops, kidney/liver) isotropic voxels, with the exception of 3 heart crops at 8\,nm. All HPF crops were imaged at 8\,nm isotropic. There is effectively zero overlap in resolution between the two preparation types. This means every Chemical-vs-HPF comparison is simultaneously a high-resolution-vs-low-resolution comparison, and the two effects cannot be separated with the available data.

To quantify this, we computed Spearman correlations between voxel size and each per-cell metric across all cells at native resolution:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
Metric & Spearman $r$ & $p$-value \\
\midrule
SA:V ratio & $-0.299$ & $8.0 \times 10^{-26}$ \\
Sphericity & $-0.166$ & $1.1 \times 10^{-8}$ \\
Curvature $|H|$ & $+0.129$ & $5.4 \times 10^{-5}$ \\
Fraction convex & $-0.071$ & $2.8 \times 10^{-2}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Crop-level metrics (ECS volume fraction, ECS width) are less directly affected since they are ratios or distances in physical units, but voxel quantization still creates artifacts at fine scales (detailed per-metric below).

%% ============================================================
\section*{Metric 1: ECS Volume Fraction}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_ecs\_cell\_volumes.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ecs\_cell\_volumes.csv}

\textbf{Plot:} \texttt{plot\_ecs\_by\_prep.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ecs\_by\_prep\_type.png}

\subsection*{What was done}
For each crop, the total number of voxels labeled as ECS and as cell (any nonzero instance ID) were counted directly from the segmentation arrays. ECS volume fraction was computed as:
\[
\text{ECS\%} = \frac{\text{ECS voxels}}{\text{total crop voxels}} \times 100
\]
No spatial analysis was needed; this is a simple label count.

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Simplest possible metric --- counting labeled voxels.
\item Least sensitive to voxel size since it is a ratio of counts at the same resolution.
\item Well-established in the EM tissue preservation literature.
\item Fast to compute (reads metadata, no heavy computation).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item One value per crop; small sample sizes (3--10 per tissue-prep group).
\item No paired anatomical regions between preps.
\item Cannot distinguish ``preparation inflated the ECS'' from ``this crop happened to land on open space.''
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Crop placement dominance:} Within Chemical fixation alone, ECS fraction ranges from 1.4\% to 74.6\% across crops. This within-group variance far exceeds any plausible between-group signal. A crop landing on a vessel lumen versus dense parenchyma can differ by 10--50$\times$.
\item \textbf{Crop size differences:} Crops range from $\sim$9M to $\sim$250M voxels. Larger crops average over more tissue heterogeneity; smaller crops are more sensitive to local features.
\item \textbf{Annotation completeness:} crop1117 has no ECS or cell labels at all (excluded from analysis). Other crops may have incomplete annotations near edges.
\item \textbf{Tissue type confound:} Each tissue has fundamentally different ECS architecture (kidney tubules vs liver sinusoids vs cortical neuropil). Cross-tissue pooling is done for statistical power but within-tissue comparisons are more meaningful.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Metric 2: ECS Channel Half-Width}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_ecs\_width\_filtered.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ecs\_width\_filtered.csv}

\textbf{Unfiltered version:} \texttt{analyze\_ecs\_width.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ecs\_width.csv}

\textbf{Plot:} \texttt{plot\_ecs\_width.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{ecs\_width\_by\_prep.png}

\subsection*{What was done}
A Euclidean distance transform was applied to the binary ECS mask for each crop, using cell voxels (any nonzero instance label) as the reference surface. Each ECS voxel received a value equal to its distance in nm to the nearest cell boundary. This gives the ``half-width'' of the ECS channel at that point --- the distance from that ECS location to the nearest cell membrane.

To remove contamination from vessel lumens, large intercellular pools, and other non-channel open spaces, values were filtered to $<$200\,nm half-width. This threshold was chosen because channels between cells are typically $<$100\,nm full width (i.e., $<$50\,nm half-width), and 200\,nm provides a generous margin.

For each crop, the following statistics were computed on the filtered ECS voxels: median, mean, standard deviation, 10th/25th/75th/90th percentiles, and cumulative fractions below 10, 20, 30, 50, 75, and 100\,nm thresholds.

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Measures the \textit{geometry} of the ECS, not just its total amount. A thin sheet of ECS and a large lumen have very different half-widths despite potentially similar volume fractions.
\item Physical units (nm) that are directly interpretable.
\item Filtering to $<$200\,nm successfully removes vessel/lumen contamination.
\item Cumulative fraction statistics give a richer distributional view than a single summary statistic.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Reports half-width, not full width. Values must be doubled for comparison with literature ``ECS width'' values.
\item The 200\,nm cutoff is somewhat arbitrary, though defensible.
\item This is a local measure --- it does not capture the connectivity or tortuosity of ECS paths.
\item The distance transform gives distance to the nearest cell surface in any direction, not specifically the gap between two opposing membranes. In complex geometries (branching ECS, pools), this overestimates the channel width.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Voxel quantization (major):} At 8\,nm voxels, the minimum possible half-width is $\sim$4\,nm (half a voxel edge) and values are quantized to $\sim$4\,nm steps. At 2\,nm voxels, resolution is 4$\times$ better. This directly affects fine-threshold statistics like frac$_{<20\text{nm}}$. This is not a theoretical concern --- it is demonstrated by the data. Within Chemical fixation alone, the same preparation type gives different results at different voxel sizes:
\end{itemize}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
Voxel & Prep & $n$ & Narrow median (nm) & frac $<$20\,nm \\
\midrule
2\,nm & Chemical  &  7 & 13.9 & 0.820 \\
4\,nm & Chemical  & 14 & 29.7 & 0.448 \\
8\,nm & Chemical  &  3 & 32.5 & 0.338 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The monotonic dependence on voxel size within Chemical fixation demonstrates that resolution dominates the signal at fine thresholds.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Crop boundary contamination:} ECS voxels at the edge of a crop see artificially large distances because there are no cell voxels beyond the boundary. The $<$200\,nm filter mitigates this for most cases.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Metric 3: Per-Cell Surface-Area-to-Volume Ratio}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_cell\_morphology.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_morphology\_per\_cell.csv}

\textbf{Normalized:} \texttt{analyze\_cell\_morphology\_normalized.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_morphology\_normalized.csv}

\textbf{Plot:} \texttt{plot\_cell\_morphology.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_morphology\_by\_prep.png}

\subsection*{What was done}
For each segmented cell (each unique nonzero ID in the instance segmentation), volume was computed as voxel count $\times$ voxel volume (in nm$^3$ and $\mu$m$^3$). Surface area was computed by face-counting on the cubic voxel grid: for each cell voxel, each of its 6 faces was checked. If the neighboring voxel was labeled as ECS, that face was counted as ECS-facing surface area. Faces abutting the crop boundary or other cells were counted separately. The SA:V ratio uses only ECS-facing surface area, in units of 1/nm.

Because face-counting on a cubic grid is sensitive to voxel size (the ``staircase effect'' --- finer voxels resolve more surface detail and produce higher surface area estimates), a resolution-normalized version was also computed. For this, all segmentation masks were resampled to 8\,nm isotropic voxels using nearest-neighbor interpolation before the same face-counting analysis. This puts all crops on equal footing with respect to voxel geometry, at the cost of smoothing fine features in the higher-resolution crops.

The native-resolution analysis used a physical volume filter of $>$2.56$\times10^6$\,nm$^3$ to exclude very small cell fragments. The normalized analysis was run with no filter and with filters at $\geq$0.005, 0.01, and 0.05\,$\mu$m$^3$.

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Per-cell metric, giving large sample sizes (1,179 cells native; 1,574 cells normalized).
\item Only counts ECS-facing faces, excluding crop boundary and cell-cell contact faces.
\item The normalized version controls for voxel-size effects.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Cells truncated at crop boundaries have distorted volumes and surface areas.
\item High within-group variance from cell-type diversity.
\item Does not directly measure ECS geometry.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Voxel staircase effect:} Face-counting on a cubic grid systematically overestimates true surface area compared to a smooth surface. The bias is larger at finer voxels (more staircase steps per unit length). This is partially controlled by normalization to 8\,nm.
\item \textbf{Boundary-truncated cells (major):} 601 of 1,179 native-resolution cells (51\%) are below 0.01\,$\mu$m$^3$. Many of these are fragments of cells that extend beyond the crop boundary, not complete cells. 158 cells have 100\% ECS-facing surface (no contact with any other cell), indicating they are isolated fragments entirely surrounded by ECS.
\item \textbf{SA:V direction reverses with size filter:} Without filtering, Chemical SA:V $>$ HPF. At $\geq$0.05\,$\mu$m$^3$ filter, Chemical SA:V $<$ HPF. The metric is unstable with respect to the minimum cell-size threshold, because small fragments (which inflate SA:V) are more common at higher resolution.
\item \textbf{Normalization introduces smoothing:} Resampling to 8\,nm blurs features that exist at 2--4\,nm, changing absolute values. Chemical sphericity jumps from 0.518 (native) to 0.650 (normalized) purely from resampling.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Metric 4: Per-Cell Sphericity}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} Same as Metric 3.

\subsection*{What was done}
Sphericity was computed for each cell as:
\[
\psi = \frac{\pi^{1/3} (6V)^{2/3}}{A}
\]
where $V$ is cell volume and $A$ is \textit{total} surface area (all 6-connected faces, including cell-cell contacts and crop boundary faces). This ranges from 0 (highly non-spherical) to 1 (perfect sphere). Unlike SA:V, sphericity uses total surface area rather than ECS-facing surface area only.

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Bounded [0,1] scale.
\item Per-cell metric with large sample sizes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Closely correlated with SA:V; partially redundant.
\item Voxel-based surface area systematically overestimates true area, so sphericity is systematically underestimated.
\item ``Sphericity'' is poorly named for cells that are never remotely spherical (neurons, hepatocytes, epithelial cells).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Voxel staircase underestimates sphericity} by inflating surface area. Coarser voxels produce a coarser staircase, but the relationship is non-trivial.
\item \textbf{Normalization dramatically changes values:} Chemical sphericity jumps from 0.518 (native, mixed 2--4\,nm) to 0.650 (normalized 8\,nm). This is pure resampling smoothing, not biology.
\item Same boundary-truncation issues as SA:V.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Results}

Normalized to 8\,nm (median sphericity, Mann--Whitney $p$):

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Filter & Chem ($n$) & HPF ($n$) & $p$ \\
\midrule
None        & 0.650 (761) & 0.601 (813) & --- \\
$\geq$0.005\,$\mu$m$^3$ & 0.570 (341) & 0.510 (472) & $<$0.0001 \\
$\geq$0.01\,$\mu$m$^3$  & 0.569 (194) & 0.482 (385) & $<$0.0001 \\

\section*{Metric 5: Inter-Cell Gap Width (Voronoi Tessellation)}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_cell\_contacts.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_contacts.csv}

\textbf{Plot:} \texttt{plot\_cell\_contacts.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_contacts\_by\_prep.png}

\subsection*{What was done}
A Voronoi tessellation of the ECS was constructed by assigning each ECS voxel to its nearest cell (using the distance transform on the cell instance mask). At Voronoi boundaries --- where two cells' territories meet --- the gap width was computed as the sum of both cells' distance contributions (i.e., twice the distance transform value at the boundary, since each side contributes half the gap).

For each crop, the median gap width was computed across all Voronoi boundary voxels. Contact fractions were also computed: at each threshold (10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320\,nm), the fraction of Voronoi boundary voxels with gap width below that threshold.

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Directly measures the actual separation between neighboring cells through the ECS.
\item The Voronoi approach naturally identifies cell neighbors without requiring explicit adjacency computation.
\item Contact fractions at multiple thresholds provide a cumulative view of the gap distribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item One value per crop; small sample sizes.
\item High within-group variance.
\item No per-cell-pair information --- these are crop-level aggregates.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Resolution floor (critical):} At 8\,nm voxels, the minimum measurable gap is approximately $2 \times$ one voxel diagonal $= 2 \times 6.9\,\text{nm} \approx 13.8\,\text{nm}$. HPF crops (all 8\,nm) physically cannot detect gaps below $\sim$14\,nm via Voronoi. This is proven by the data:
\end{itemize}

\centering

\toprule

Chemical 8\,nm crops show the same 0\% contact at 20\,nm as HPF 8\,nm crops. The Chemical-vs-HPF difference at 20\,nm is entirely a voxel resolution artifact.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Open-space contamination:} Vessel lumens and large intercellular pools create wide Voronoi boundaries that inflate gap measurements.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Metric 6: Boundary-Based Contact Fraction (Discarded)}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_cell\_morphology.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_morphology\_contacts.csv}

\subsection*{What was done}
For each crop, at each distance threshold (10, 20, 40, 80, 160\,nm), we asked: does any pair of cell boundary voxels come within that distance of each other? The fraction of crops where this is true was reported.

\subsection*{Why it was discarded}
This metric returned 1.000 (100\%) for every crop at every threshold $\geq$20\,nm. At 10\,nm, it returned 0.000 for all HPF crops and 1.000 for all Chemical crops (a pure resolution artifact --- 10\,nm is slightly more than one voxel at 8\,nm). The metric has zero discriminating power. It effectively asks ``do any cells exist near each other in this crop?'' which is trivially true for any crop containing multiple cells.

%% ============================================================
\section*{Metric 7: Surface Curvature --- Voxel-Based}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_curvature.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_curvature.csv}

\subsection*{What was done}
For each cell passing the volume filter ($>$2.56$\times10^6$\,nm$^3$), a bounding box was extracted from the binary cell mask. The mask was converted to a float array and smoothed with a 3D Gaussian kernel with $\sigma = 16$\,nm in physical units ($\sigma = 8$ voxels at 2\,nm, 4 voxels at 4\,nm, 2 voxels at 8\,nm).

Mean curvature $H$ was computed at each surface voxel using the level-set formula:
\[
H = \frac{(\phi_{xx}(\phi_y^2 + \phi_z^2) + \phi_{yy}(\phi_x^2 + \phi_z^2) + \phi_{zz}(\phi_x^2 + \phi_y^2)) - 2(\phi_x\phi_y\phi_{xy} + \phi_x\phi_z\phi_{xz} + \phi_y\phi_z\phi_{yz})}{2(\phi_x^2 + \phi_y^2 + \phi_z^2)^{3/2}}
\]
where $\phi$ is the smoothed mask and subscripts denote partial derivatives computed via finite differences.

Surface voxels were identified as cell voxels with at least one non-cell neighbor. Only ECS-facing surface voxels (those neighboring an ECS-labeled voxel) were retained. Per-cell statistics: median $|H|$, mean $|H|$, standard deviation, 25th/75th percentiles, fraction convex ($H > 0$), fraction concave ($H < 0$), and median radius of curvature ($1/|H|$).

Sign convention: positive $H$ = convex (cell surface bulging outward into ECS); negative $H$ = concave (invagination or wrapping around a neighbor).

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Characterizes membrane shape at a local level.
\item Fixed physical smoothing scale (16\,nm) in principle makes curvature comparable across resolutions.
\item Per-cell metric with large sample sizes (425 Chemical, 549 HPF cells).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Computing curvature from finite differences on a binary voxel grid is fundamentally noisy.
\item The smoothing scale choice (16\,nm) directly controls results.
\item Mean curvature alone does not distinguish all surface shape types (would need Gaussian curvature for a full classification).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Voxel staircase noise:} Surface normals computed from finite differences on a cubic grid produce quantization artifacts.
\item \textbf{Smoothing scale vs resolution:} 16\,nm $= 8$ voxels at 2\,nm but only 2 voxels at 8\,nm. HPF data receives much less effective smoothing, potentially retaining more voxel staircase noise.
\item \textbf{Overwhelmingly concave (85--87\%):} This likely reflects overrepresentation of concave junctions in the voxel-based surface normal computation, and aliasing of flat surfaces as concave.
\item \textbf{Superseded:} The mesh-based curvature (Metric 8) eliminates the worst voxel staircase artifacts and is preferred.
\end{itemize}

%% ============================================================
\section*{Metric 8: Surface Curvature --- Mesh-Based}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Script:} \texttt{analyze\_curvature\_mesh.py} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{cell\_curvature\_mesh.csv}

\subsection*{What was done}
For each cell passing the same volume filter, a triangulated isosurface was extracted from the binary cell mask using the marching cubes algorithm (\texttt{skimage.measure.marching\_cubes}), with 1-voxel Gaussian pre-smoothing applied to the binary mask to regularize the surface. The resulting mesh was a set of vertices and triangular faces in physical coordinates (nm).

Mean curvature $H$ was computed at each mesh vertex using the discrete Laplace--Beltrami operator (\texttt{trimesh.curvature.discrete\_mean\_curvature\_measure}). This computes curvature from the local geometry of the triangle mesh, which is geometrically more meaningful than finite differences on a binary grid.

To restrict analysis to ECS-facing surfaces, each mesh vertex was tested against the full segmentation volume: the nearest non-self voxel label was looked up, and only vertices where this label was ECS (rather than another cell ID) were retained. This was done using a KD-tree lookup in the segmentation volume.

Per-cell statistics were computed identically to the voxel-based method. Additionally, mesh quality metadata was recorded: total vertex count, total face count, and number of ECS-facing vertices.

\subsection*{Strengths}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Marching cubes produces a smooth triangulated surface, eliminating the worst voxel staircase artifacts.
\item Curvature from vertex normals on a proper triangle mesh is geometrically more meaningful.
\item Mesh quality metadata (vertex/face counts) allows quality filtering.
\item Without size filtering, Chemical and HPF curvature are nearly identical (median $|H|$ = 0.0107 vs 0.0105), suggesting this method is more stable across resolutions than the voxel-based method.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Weaknesses}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Marching cubes plus Gaussian smoothing acts as a low-pass filter. The amount of smoothing determines which features survive.
\item Curvature magnitudes are $\sim$2$\times$ higher than the voxel-based method. It is unclear whether this reflects real high-curvature features or mesh triangulation noise.
\item Computationally expensive (mesh extraction + per-vertex curvature + KD-tree lookup for ECS classification).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Biases and artifacts}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item \textbf{Implicit smoothing:} Marching cubes inherently smooths the voxel surface. Additional 1-voxel Gaussian smoothing further regularizes. The net smoothing is resolution-dependent (1 voxel = 2\,nm or 8\,nm depending on dataset).
\item \textbf{Still concave-biased (82\%):} Less extreme than voxel-based (87\%) but still dominated by concavity. Some concavity is real biology (cells wrap around each other at contact points). But the magnitude is likely inflated by ECS-vertex selection bias --- ECS-facing vertices preferentially sample membrane at invaginations and concavities where cells separate, rather than flat contact surfaces which are classified as non-ECS.
\item \textbf{Mesh resolution varies:} Chemical cells have median 26,198 mesh vertices vs 14,009 for HPF, because finer voxels produce denser meshes. It is unknown whether this affects curvature convergence.
\item \textbf{Method disagreement:} Mesh median $|H|$ is $\sim$2$\times$ the voxel-based value (0.0107 vs 0.0065 for Chemical). Mesh radius of curvature is $\sim$60\% smaller (94\,nm vs 153\,nm). This could mean the mesh resolves real high-curvature features that voxel smoothing missed, or that mesh triangulation artifacts inflate curvature at sharp edges.
\end{itemize}
